Section 1

Power balance model

The fundamental assumption is that, at a given instant, all mechanical power produced by the rider is consumed by three resistance forces: aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance, and gravity.

Total power equation

P = P_aero + P_rolling + P_gravity P_aero = ½ · ρ · CdA · v³ P_rolling = m · g · Crr · v P_gravity = m · g · (grade/100) · v
SymbolQuantityUnitTypical value
PMechanical power outputW—
ρAir densitykg/m³1.2 (sea level, 20 °C)
CdAAerodynamic drag aream²0.20–0.40
vGround speedm/s—
mTotal system mass (rider + bike)kg70–100
gGravitational accelerationm/s²9.81
CrrRolling resistance coefficient—0.003–0.005
gradeRoad gradient%—

Air density is fixed at 1.2 kg/m³. This is a reasonable approximation at sea level in temperate conditions. Altitude, temperature, and humidity all affect ρ; for high-altitude testing, this should be corrected.

Wind speed is not measured. The model assumes still air — any headwind or tailwind will directly bias the CdA result. For reliable measurements, riding in both directions and averaging is recommended.

Section 2

CdA inversion from ride data

Given a recorded segment with known average power, speed, and grade, the power balance equation is rearranged to solve for CdA:

# Effective power after drivetrain losses P_eff = P × η # Subtract gravitational and rolling components P_aero = P_eff − m·g·v·(grade/100) − m·g·v·Crr # Solve for CdA CdA = 2 · P_aero / (ρ · v³)

Where η is the drivetrain efficiency entered by the user (typically 0.97 for a clean road drivetrain). This accounts for mechanical losses in the chain, bearings, and derailleur pulleys before power reaches the rear wheel.

Floor at zero: If the computed P_aero is negative (e.g. on a steep descent where gravity dominates), CdA is clamped to 0. This prevents physically nonsensical negative drag values for segments where gravity and rolling resistance already exceed total power output.

Segment-level computation

CdA is computed independently for each aggregated segment (approximately 20 seconds of data). Segments where average speed falls below 2 m/s are excluded — at very low speeds, the aerodynamic term is negligible and numerical noise dominates.

The reported CdA for a selected section of the ride is the value computed from the time-weighted averages of that entire window, not a mean of per-segment values.

Section 3

Speed solver

The inverse problem — given a target power and road grade, what speed will the rider reach? — is used by the GPX route modelling feature. There is no closed-form solution to the cubic power equation, so Hopkins uses a stepped iterative solver:

# Increase speed in coarse steps until power is exceeded v = 0 while P(v) < P_target: v += 1.0 # Back off one coarse step, then refine at 0.1 m/s v -= 1.0 while P(v) < P_target: v += 0.1 # Back off and refine further at 0.01 m/s v -= 0.1 while P(v) < P_target: v += 0.01

The result is accurate to within 0.01 m/s (0.036 km/h). A maximum speed cap is applied: if the solver exceeds the user-defined maximum speed, that cap is returned instead (relevant on long descents).

The three-pass refinement avoids the overhead of a full bisection while providing sufficient precision for pacing purposes.

Section 4

FIT file parsing pipeline

Coordinate conversion

Garmin FIT files store GPS coordinates in semicircles, an integer representation of angle. To convert to decimal degrees:

latitude_deg = position_lat / 11,930,465 longitude_deg = position_long / 11,930,465

The constant 11,930,465 is derived from 2³¹ / 180 — the number of semicircle units per degree.

Micro-segment construction

Each consecutive pair of fitpoints defines one micro-segment:

time = t₂ − t₁ # seconds distance = d₂ − d₁ # metres (cumulative device distance) altitude = alt₂ − alt₁ # metres gain grade = altitude / distance × 100 # percent avg_speed = distance / time # m/s

Power and heart rate are taken from the start point of each micro-segment (the FIT record is instantaneous, so no interpolation is applied).

Temporal aggregation

Groups of N consecutive micro-segments (default N = 20) are merged into a single aggregated segment. Time-sensitive quantities (HR, cadence, power) are weighted by the duration of each micro-segment:

avg_power = Σ(powerᵢ × timeᵢ) / Σ(timeᵢ)

Distance and altitude are summed directly. The first and last N points of each activity are discarded to avoid edge artifacts.

Lap extraction

Lap markers are stored as separate lap messages in the FIT file. Each lap has a start_time and timestamp (end time). These are matched against fitpoint timestamps to produce point-index boundaries, which are then rendered as clickable bands in the activity view.

Section 5

GPX route modelling

The GPX modelling pipeline is a separate, forward simulation mode. Rather than extracting CdA from recorded power, it takes a GPX route file and a set of rider parameters, and predicts finish time and pacing across the course.

Haversine distance

GPS coordinates in the GPX file (decimal degrees) are converted to ground distance using the Haversine formula, which accounts for the spherical geometry of the Earth (radius R = 6,371,000 m):

Δφ = φ₂ − φ₁ Δλ = λ₂ − λ₁ a = sin²(Δφ/2) + cos(φ₁)·cos(φ₂)·sin²(Δλ/2) c = 2 · atan2(√a, √(1−a)) d = R · c

Elevation change between consecutive GPX points is taken directly from the elevation field. Grade in ‰ (per mille) is computed over 100 m windows to smooth GPS elevation noise:

grade_permille = round(Δelevation / distance × 1000)

Grade binning

For pacing optimisation, the route is reduced to eight terrain bins by gradient. Each bin accumulates total distance, and a different power target is assigned to each bin:

Bins (grade in ‰): 0 → < −30 (steep descent) 1 → −30 to 0 (gentle descent / false flat) 2 → 0 to 10 (near flat) 3 → 10 to 20 (slight uphill) 4 → 20 to 30 (moderate climb) 5 → 30 to 50 (sustained climb) 6 → 50 to 80 (steep climb) 7 → ≥ 80 (very steep)

Dual-CdA model

Cyclists adopt different body positions depending on terrain. On flat or slightly rolling terrain, a rider typically holds an aerodynamic (low) position; on climbs, they sit upright. The model accepts two CdA values:

cda_low # aerodynamic position (flat/descent bins 1–3) cda_base # upright position (climbing bins 4–7 and steep descents)

For bins 0 (steep descent), 4, 5, 6, and 7 (moderate to steep climbs), cda_base is used. For bins 1–3 (slight downhill to moderate uphill), cda_low is applied. This reflects the real-world practice of staying aero on the flats but sitting up on climbs.

Pacing simulation

Given a target normalised power (NP) and a power distribution across the eight bins, the simulator computes elapsed time per bin using the speed solver described in Section 3. A renormalisation loop adjusts the per-bin power distribution to match the target NP while minimising total time — effectively solving a constrained optimisation problem over the pacing strategy.

Deployment note: The GPX simulation feature is implemented in the codebase but is not yet exposed as a user-facing page. The physics described in this section reflects the current implementation.
Section 6

Assumptions and limitations

AssumptionImpact if violated
Zero wind Any ambient wind directly shifts CdA. A 1 m/s headwind at 40 km/h causes roughly +0.02 m² error. Ride out-and-back and average to partially cancel this.
ρ = 1.2 kg/m³ Air density varies ±5% between sea level and 1000 m, and with temperature. Correct for altitude: ρ ≈ 1.2 × exp(−altitude / 8500).
Uniform Crr Rolling resistance varies with surface, tyre pressure, and temperature. Use a value appropriate for your tyres and conditions.
Power meter accuracy Most power meters are accurate to ±2%. This translates to roughly ±0.005 m² CdA at typical road speeds.
GPS altitude accuracy Barometric altitude (when available) is more accurate than GPS altitude. Grade errors affect the gravity subtraction term. Use flat roads to minimise this source of error.
Steady-state speed The model uses segment-average speed and power. Rapid acceleration within a segment violates the steady-state assumption. Select smooth, constant-pace efforts.
